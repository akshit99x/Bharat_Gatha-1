| `NACHCHIYAR-0001` | The Chola Country · The Pot Ballot | Before we draw, the rules are read aloud. They are not in a book that can be edited — they are cut into that wall, where everyone can check them. Sabha business, Uttaramerur, in the reign of Parantaka. Shall I read the qualifications or the disqualifications first? |
| `NACHCHIYAR-0002` | The Chola Country · The Pot Ballot | A candidate must own a certain extent of tax-paying land, live in a house on his own site, be between roughly thirty-five and seventy years of age, and know the Vedas or at least the prescribed texts. Note what that means: there is a property qualification and a literacy qualification. This is not universal suffrage. It is a rule-bound, written, auditable local government — which in the tenth century is remarkable enough. |
| `NACHCHIYAR-0003` | The Chola Country · The Pot Ballot | Barred: anyone who has not submitted his accounts; anyone whose relatives have not submitted theirs; anyone who took the committee seat in the last three years; anyone convicted of certain offences; anyone who rode the village’s business for private gain. Audit first, then eligibility. The inscription is blunt about it. |
| `NACHCHIYAR-0004` | The Chola Country · The Pot Ballot | That is the sentence I would carve. |
| `NACHCHIYAR-0005` | The Chola Country · The Pot Ballot | Each ward writes its eligible names on palm-leaf tickets. All tickets go into a pot — a kudam. A young boy who cannot read, so he cannot favour anyone, draws one ticket per ward in full view of the assembly. We call it kudavolai, the pot-ticket. Terms do not repeat for three years. Then the accounts are read again next year, and the wall does not forget. |
